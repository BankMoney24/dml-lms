## Current, voltage and resistance

An electric **current** (I) is a flow of charge. We measure it in **amperes** (A); small currents are often given in **milliamperes** (mA). 1 A = 1000 mA.

The **voltage** or potential difference (V) across a component is the energy each unit of charge gives up as it passes through. We measure it in **volts** (V).

**Resistance** (R) is how strongly a component opposes the current. We measure it in **ohms** (Ω).

## Ohm's law

For a metal conductor kept at a steady temperature, the current through it is proportional to the voltage across it:

```text
V = I × R        so        R = V ÷ I        and        I = V ÷ R
```

Double the voltage and the current doubles. A graph of current against voltage is a straight line through the origin, and its gradient tells you the resistance.

> **Tip:** Always change mA to A before you use the formula. 25 mA = 0.025 A.

## Measuring correctly

- An **ammeter** measures current. It goes **in series**, so the same current flows through it and the component.
- A **voltmeter** measures voltage. It goes **in parallel** (across) the component you are interested in.

A real cell has a small **internal resistance**, so the voltage across the resistor is a little less than the cell's setting. That is why you read the voltmeter instead of trusting the number on the power supply.

## Worked example

A voltmeter across a resistor reads 4.50 V and the ammeter reads 150 mA.

1. Change the current to amperes: 150 mA = 0.150 A.
2. R = V ÷ I = 4.50 ÷ 0.150 = **30 Ω**.
3. At 6.0 V across the same resistor: I = 6.0 ÷ 30 = 0.20 A = **200 mA**.

## Why take several readings?

One reading can hide a mistake: a meter misread, a loose wire, a slip on the calculator. Take at least three readings at different voltages. If each gives nearly the same value of V ÷ I, you can trust your answer; plotting them on a V–I graph shows it at a glance.
