## The simple pendulum

A simple pendulum is a small heavy **bob** on a light thread, swinging through a small angle. One **oscillation** is a complete swing: from one side, across, and back to where it started.

The **period**, T, is the time for one oscillation. For small swings it depends only on the length, l, and the acceleration due to gravity, g:

```text
T = 2π √(l ÷ g)
```

It does not depend on the mass of the bob, and hardly at all on how far it swings, as long as the angle stays small (under about 10°).

## A straight-line graph from a curve

Square both sides:

```text
T² = (4π² ÷ g) × l
```

So a graph of **T² against l** is a straight line through the origin, with

```text
slope = 4π² ÷ g        and so        g = 4π² ÷ slope
```

> **Tip:** Measure l in **metres** for the graph, so the slope comes out in s²/m and g in m/s².

## Taking good readings

- Measure the length from the point of suspension to the **centre** of the bob.
- Time **20 oscillations**, not one. Your reaction time (about 0.2 s) then matters 20 times less.
- Start timing as the bob passes the middle of its swing, where it moves fastest and is easiest to judge.
- Use at least **five lengths**, spread out, and tabulate l, t, T = t ÷ 20 and T².

## Worked example

At l = 0.80 m, 20 oscillations take 35.9 s.

1. T = 35.9 ÷ 20 = 1.795 s, so T² = 3.22 s².
2. Suppose the best line through all the points has slope 4.03 s²/m.
3. g = 4π² ÷ 4.03 = 39.48 ÷ 4.03 = **9.80 m/s²**.

g is not quite the same everywhere on Earth: it is a little smaller near the equator than near the poles. In the lab, your g is set for your session, so read it from your own graph.
