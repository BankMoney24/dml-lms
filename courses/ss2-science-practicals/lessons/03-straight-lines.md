## The equation of a straight line

Every straight line (except a vertical one) can be written as

```text
y = mx + c
```

- **m** is the **gradient**: how much y goes up for every 1 that x goes along. A negative gradient means the line goes down from left to right.
- **c** is the **intercept**: the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis (where x = 0).

## Finding the gradient from two points

Pick any two points on the line, (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂):

```text
m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)        change in y ÷ change in x
```

Example: the points (1, 5) and (3, 9).

- m = (9 − 5) ÷ (3 − 1) = 4 ÷ 2 = **2**.

## Finding the intercept

If the line crosses the y-axis at a point you can read, that is c. Otherwise put one point into y = mx + c and solve:

- Using (1, 5) with m = 2: 5 = 2 × 1 + c, so **c = 3**.
- The line is **y = 2x + 3**.

## Using the equation

Once you know m and c you can find y for any x, even far off the graph. For x = 10: y = 2 × 10 + 3 = **23**.

> **Tip:** Check your equation with a third point. If (3, 9) is on the line, then 2 × 3 + 3 should equal 9. It does.

## Fractions and negatives

Gradients are often fractions such as 1.5 or −0.5. Take care with signs: going from (2, 4) to (6, 2) the change in y is 2 − 4 = −2 and the change in x is 4, so m = −2 ÷ 4 = −0.5.
